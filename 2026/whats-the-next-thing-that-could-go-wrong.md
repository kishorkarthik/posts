# What's the Next Thing That Can Go Wrong?

Lately, I’ve been paying closer attention to what happens right before I make a decision or when I'm working toward something.

There’s often a subtle moment of hesitation. It isn't a dramatic crisis—just a quiet, familiar second-guessing that asks: *What if this doesn't turn out the way I expect?*

That hesitation easily spirals into overthinking. Once it takes hold, it's easy to get pulled into replaying different scenarios over and over. But overthinking is deceptively passive. It feels like effort because it consumes mental energy, yet it rarely produces a single concrete decision.

When I tried to counter that habit with standard advice—telling myself to "think positive" or assuming that everything would work out fine—it never felt particularly convincing. Forced optimism is surprisingly fragile. If your peace of mind relies on everything going smoothly, even a minor setback can throw you off balance. You haven't really built confidence; you've just postponed dealing with reality.

A while back, I came across a line in Chris Hadfield’s memoir that gave me a much better way to think about this. He described a question astronauts ask themselves continuously in space: *"What’s the next thing that could kill me?"*

It sounds intense, but the value of the question has nothing to do with extreme stakes—it’s about agency. In a crisis, the "why" is irrelevant. You need to accept where you find yourself and prioritize what matters right in that moment. There is plenty of time later to figure out the why. What matters is asking: what is the next thing that could go wrong? Focusing on that single next thing, whatever it is, is how you stay on track.

You can see this same pattern in how Flight Director Gene Kranz handled the Apollo 13 crisis. When the oxygen tank exploded, the control room didn't navigate the situation through blind optimism or panicked guesses. When people started speculating about sensor glitches or meteorites, Kranz stepped in with a line that applies just as well to everyday choices: *"Let's work the problem, people. Let's not make things worse by guessing. From this moment on we are improvising a new mission"*

Instead of offering generic reassurance, he forced everyone to move from speculation to observation, and from observation to action.

In practical terms, that approach comes down to three basic steps: **warn, gather, work**.

First, be honest when something isn't working rather than trying to downplay it. Second, gather actual facts before reacting—separating what is demonstrably happening from what you're merely imagining. Third, work on the immediate next step rather than getting overwhelmed by the entire outcome.

Asking "What's the next thing that can go wrong?" isn't about being pessimistic. It's about moving from passive hope to active focus. Instead of hoping that nothing goes sideways, you simply give yourself a clear plan for when it does.

Real confidence doesn't come from expecting everything to go smoothly. It comes from knowing that when things don't go as expected, you don't have to panic or guess. You can break the problem down into manageable pieces and handle them one step at a time.

So when that familiar hesitation pops up, there's no need to push it away or cover it up with forced positivity. Just ask the question: *What’s the next thing that can go wrong?*

Then figure out the next step, and keep moving forward.
